Model Idea:

I believe that there is a correlation between the rankings of the ratios of goals scored to goals conceded for each Premier League team in a season and that same season's actual final ranking table. The reasoning behind this approach is that a team that consistently scores more goals than it concedes is likely to be more successful. The idea of my model is to be able to predict the total number of goals scored by each team over a single season, then do the same for goals conceded, calculate the ratio of these two (total goals scored divided by total goals conceded), and then finally rank these teams from highest ratio to lowest ratio which will be our prediction for the final league table for the 2026/27 Premier League season. By comparing the predicted goal ratios of all teams, I hope to identify a relationship between goal ratio and final league position. However, because the official Premier League table is determined directly by points, the rankings produced by my model may differ from the actual table.

Data Usage:

I used a large dataset containing many columns showing lots of different statistics in each game from the 2014/15 Premier League season up to the 2026/27 Premier League season. However, I decided to only use a small group of columns to make my model work. These chosen columns included information on who was the home and away team in a match, the full time home and away goals in a match, and finally the date of each match. I used the first 11 seasons (the 2014/15 season up to and including the 2024/25 season) as training data to train my model and then used the 2025/26 season as my test data. I want to predict the 2026/27 season but as there is already some data available for this season, I did implement the first 6 games of this season to aid the prediction for the entire 38 game season.

Model Training:

After selecting the relevant columns from the dataset, I used my code to process the match results into statistics for each team. I then used these statistics to create eight features that the model uses to make predictions. The model is trained using historical data at different points in each season: after 6, 10, 15, 20 and 25 matches. This allows it to learn how a team's performance earlier in a season relates to its final goals scored and conceded. These checkpoints also allow the model to learn from each team's statistics at different stages of the season. I used two Random Forest regression models, with the first predicting each team's final total goals scored and the second predicting its final total goals conceded.

Making Predictions:

After training the models, I used them to predict each team's final goals scored and goals conceded for the 2026/27 season. I used the results from the first 6 matches, along with statistics from the previous season, to help make these predictions. I then calculated each team's predicted goal ratio by dividing their predicted goals scored by their predicted goals conceded. Finally, I sorted the teams from the highest predicted goal ratio to the lowest to produce my predicted rankings for the 2026/27 Premier League season.

Testing:

To test my model, I used the 2025/26 season as test data and compared the predicted goal ratio rankings with the actual goal ratio ranking. My code calculated the absolute rank error for each team, which shows how many places the predicted goal ratio ranking was away from the actual goal ratio ranking. It also calculated the mean absolute rank error across all teams, as well as how many teams were predicted in exactly the correct goal ratio ranking or within one or two places of their correct goal ratio ranking. It also displayed how many teams were predicted over 5 places away from the correct goal ratio ranking. This helped me evaluate how accurately my model predicted the goal ratio rankings, which may provide an indication of the final league standings

Limitations:

The main limitation of my model is that its accuracy depends heavily on the strength of the relationship between goal ratio and the actual Premier League table ranking. This means that in theory, my model could correctly predict the final goal ratios for all 20 teams but could still get the final table wrong because there may be instances where one team has a worse goal ratio than another team, yet still finishes above them in the Premier League table. Another limitation is that my model uses the first 6 games of the season it is trying to predict. This means that my model would not be able to predict the season before it had started and therefore relies on knowing at least some information about the season before predicting the final results over all 38 games.

Conclusion:

In conclusion, my model uses historical Premier League data and the first 6 matches of the 2026/27 season to predict each team's final goals scored and goals conceded. It then calculates the predicted goal ratio for each team and ranks them from highest to lowest. By testing the model on the 2025/26 season, I was able to evaluate how accurately it predicted the final goal ratio rankings by analysing the absolute rank errors. Given how unpredictable football can be, with some teams unexpectedly overperforming or underperforming, I believe my model has the potential to make useful predictions. However, its overall accuracy should be judged by the results of my testing. The model also has limitations because goal ratio does not always match the official league table, which is determined primarily by points. Its predictions may also be affected by unexpected changes in team performance throughout the season. Overall, the model provides a way to use historical data and results early in the season to predict team performance, although its rankings should be treated as predictions rather than a guaranteed final league table.
