My model aims to predict the final rankings of all 20 competing Premier League teams using the ratio between the total number of goals scored and the total number of goals conceded for each team. The idea behind this is that teams that consistently score more goals than they concede are generally more successful. A relevant paper I read called "Football Match Result Prediction Using the Random Forest Classifier" published in August 2019 and written by Pakawan Pugsee and Pattarachai Pattawong investigates the use of machine learning to predict Premier League football match results using a Random Forest Classifier. They trained their model using historical data from 3 previous seasons, considering factors such as previous match results and home and away performance. Their model achieved an overall prediction accuracy of 80% when it was tested on 220 matches from the 2017/18 season, demonstrating the potential of Random Forest models for predicting football outcomes. However, they did notice that the model was less successful at predicting draws compared to home and away wins. The findings of this paper demonstrate the potential of Random Forest algorithms in football prediction, providing inspiration for my decision to use Random Forest regression models in my own project. Although their model predicts individual match results, my model takes a different approach by predicting each team's total goals scored and conceded over an entire season. To develop my model, I used historical Premier League match data from the 2014/15 season up to the first 6 matches of the 2026/27 season, selecting the home and away teams, full-time home and away goals and match dates. I used the first 11 seasons, from 2014/15 to 2024/25, as training data and the 2025/26 season as test data. After processing the results into team statistics, I created eight features for two Random Forest regression models, with one predicting each team's final total goals scored and the other predicting their final total goals conceded. The models were trained using data from different stages of historical seasons, specifically after 6, 10, 15, 20 and 25 matches, allowing them to learn how performance in the early stages of a season relates to the final goal totals. I then used the first 6 matches of the 2026/27 season, alongside statistics from the previous season, to predict each team's final goals scored and conceded over the full 38-game season. By dividing predicted goals scored by predicted goals conceded, I calculated each team's predicted goal ratio and ranked all 20 teams from highest to lowest to produce my predicted Premier League table. To evaluate the model, I tested it on the 2025/26 season by comparing its predicted goal ratio rankings with the actual goal ratio rankings. I calculated the absolute rank error for each team, the mean absolute rank error across all teams, and the number of teams predicted within 1 or 2 places of their actual ranking, as well as those more than 5 places away. This allowed me to assess how accurately the model could predict goal ratio rankings. However, the model has some limitations, with the main one being that a team's goal ratio ranking does not necessarily correspond to its actual final Premier League position, as the official table is determined primarily by points. Therefore, even if the model accurately predicts every team's goal ratio, it may still produce an inaccurate final table. Additionally, because the model uses the first 6 matches of the 2026/27 season, it cannot make a prediction before the season begins and relies on early-season results being available. Unexpected changes in team performance throughout the season may also affect its accuracy. Overall, my model uses historical data and early-season performance to predict final team goal totals and goal ratio rankings, providing an indication of potential league positions, although its predictions should not be treated as a guaranteed final table.
